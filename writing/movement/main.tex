\section{Review of methods and literature}

\noindent An affine transformation matrix in 3D space $\mathbf{T}$ has the form:

$$\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{R} & \mathbf{t} \\
\mathbf{0}^T & 1
\end{bmatrix}$$

\noindent $\mathbf{T}$ is a 4x4 matrix with submatrix $\mathbf{R}$ a 3x3 rotation matrix and vector $\mathbf{t}$ a 3x1 translation vector. The rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}$ is a member of a group called $SO(n)$ or $SE(n)$ (\textit{or possibly both at the same time, still figuring it out}) and is constrained to be orthogonal ($\mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$) and have determinate 1 ($det(\mathbf{R}) = 1$) (\textit{and potentially have unit vectors for the columns and rows, still figuring it out}). \textbf{The overall goal is to model the affine matrices that define the change in location for each tooth.} \\

\subsection{Possible approaches}

\subsubsection{Lie group regression}

Because affine transformation matrices have a certain form with specific characteristics, they form what is called a Lie group and can be operated on with Lie algebra. Lie regression ensures that the response of the model always fits the constraints of an affine transformation. There seem to be two general paths for this type of regression: modeling affine transformations via other affine transformation (or other matrices) and modeling affine transformations via scalar covariates. The latter is more in line with what I am familiar with and seems like a good place to start. The former also seems interesting, but would perhaps be easier to understand once I understand the scalar covariate modeling. However, it is possible that they share much of the same framework and understanding one is essentially the same as understanding the other. 

Here are a few papers I like that discuss Lie group regression with scalar predictors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \href{https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01621459.2016.1208615#abstract}{Lin 2016}, Extrinsic Local Regression on Manifold-Valued Data
    \item \href{https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-45026-1_7}{Porikli 2016}, Regression on Lie groups and its application to affine motion tracking (essentially the same paper as \href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221362444_Learning_on_lie_groups_for_invariant_detection_and_tracking}{Tuzel 2008})
\end{itemize}

Though I do not completely understand the math behind it yet, a common theme in these methods is projecting the matrix into a different space and then transforming it back. I have seen this done with a matrix-log/matrix-exponential approach and also a less straightforward embedding map. 

One of the main applications of Lie regression is motion tracking. This generally fits with what we are trying to do and leads me to believe it is worth investigating further. It also uses a modeling framework that is familiar in statistics which makes it conceptually appealing for method extension.

My idea for injecting novelty into this method is adapting it for use with the correlated movement we see in teeth. At this stage, I cannot say for certain that no one has worked on this model with correlated responses, but it seems like an area that could be worth pursuing or pursuing in a way that has not yet been approached. 

As Grant has suggested in the past, starting with a simplified, simulated version of the data is a good starting point. In my head, I am imagining working this out in 2D space this triangles. The first step would be understanding the methods as they currently stand. We could experiment with simulated situations like in Fig. \ref{basic2dAffine} using a basic indicator covariate and obvious differences between the transformations for the groups. Once we get to a point where we are comfortable extending the method, we could simulate a simplified version of the correlated movement we expect in orthodontic treatment (Fig. \ref{corr2dAffine}).

\subsubsection{Regression on rotation matrix and translation vector separately}

This idea simplifies things a little but why not model things together if possible? Also, if there is any relationship between the translation vector and the rotation matrix, this would not capture that. Here are some resources:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \href{https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cjs.5550340201}{Rivest 2009, Regression and correlation for 3x3 rotation matrices}
\end{itemize}

The affine transformation matrix can also be decomposed into a rotation, scaling, and shear matrix: $T = RSH$. Parameters of each could be regressed separately. Additionally, an affine transformation can be minimally represented as Euler angles, an axis angle, a rotation vector, and a quaternion. Again, each piece could be handled separately.
