\section{Review of methods and literature}

\subsection{Rigid motion}

\noindent Rigid motion in $n$-D space is defined by an affine transformation matrix $\mathbf{T}$ with the form:

$$\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{R} & \mathbf{t} \\
\mathbf{0}^T & 1
\end{bmatrix}$$


\noindent $\mathbf{T}$ is a $(n+1)$ x $(n+1)$ matrix with submatrix $\mathbf{R}$ a $n$ x $n$ rotation matrix and vector $\mathbf{t}$ a $n$ x 1 translation vector. $\mathbf{T}$ is a member of SE(n) and the rotation submatrix $\mathbf{R}$ is a member of $SO(n)$. $\mathbf{R}$ is constrained to be orthogonal ($\mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$) and have determinate 1 ($det(\mathbf{R}) = 1$). \textbf{The applied goal is to model the affine transformation matrices defining the rigid motion of teeth in orthodontic treatment. The methodological goal is to introduce novel modeling strategies for constrained matrix responses, especially those in $\mathbf{SE}(\mathbf{n})$.} \\

\section{Lie groups and Lie algebras}

\subsection{Basics and why we care}

Rigid transformations (along with many other sets of constrained matrices like positive semi-definite and rotation) are a set of matrices that form a manifold. A manifold is just a smooth surface in space that is locally flat. This set of matrices forms a manifold because there are no ``holes" in the set. If you look between any two matrices in the set, there exists another matrix that is part of the set. Thus, a surface.

In addition to the set of rigid transformations (and many other constrained matrix sets) forming a manifold, this set is also a Lie group. Being a Lie group introduces certain properties, most important of which for this work is the associated Lie algebra. The Lie algebra is the space tangent to the Lie group at the identity. Conveniently, the Lie algebra is a vector space which means it is easy to work on. Elements of the Lie group can be projected up to the Lie algebra, worked on in a vector space environment, and then projected back down to the Lie group. The resulting element projected back down to the Lie group will always be a member of the Lie group.

\subsection{Relationship between Lie group and algebra}

The matrix exponential maps the Lie algebra to the Lie group. The exponential map is onto but not one-to-one. The matrix logarithm maps the Lie group to the Lie algebra. The logarithm map is only uniquely defined around the identity, which introduces some problems as matrices get farther from this point.

Define $\mathbf{G}$ to be a Lie group and $\mathbf{g}$ to be the associated Lie algebra. Let $\mathbf{T}$ be a member of the Lie group and $\mathbf{t}$ be a member of the Lie algebra. The mapping is defined as:

\begin{gather*}
    exp(\mathbf{t}) = \mathbf{T} \\
    log(\mathbf{T}) = \mathbf{t}.
\end{gather*}

The details of the non-one-to-one-ness is something I am still figuring out. However, the important part of this relationship is that since the Lie algebra is defined at the identity of the Lie group, members of the Lie group farther from the identity have problems. This comes up over and over again in the literature.

\subsubsection{Lie group regression}

Because affine transformation matrices have a certain form with specific characteristics, they form what is called a Lie group and can be operated on with Lie algebra. Lie regression ensures that the response of the model always fits the constraints of an affine transformation. There seem to be two general paths for this type of regression: modeling affine transformations via other affine transformation (or other matrices) and modeling affine transformations via scalar covariates. The latter is more in line with what I am familiar with and seems like a good place to start. The former also seems interesting, but would perhaps be easier to understand once I understand the scalar covariate modeling. However, it is possible that they share much of the same framework and understanding one is essentially the same as understanding the other. 

Here are a few papers I like that discuss Lie group regression with scalar predictors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \href{https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01621459.2016.1208615#abstract}{Lin 2016}, Extrinsic Local Regression on Manifold-Valued Data
    \item \href{https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-45026-1_7}{Porikli 2016}, Regression on Lie groups and its application to affine motion tracking (essentially the same paper as \href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221362444_Learning_on_lie_groups_for_invariant_detection_and_tracking}{Tuzel 2008})
\end{itemize}

Though I do not completely understand the math behind it yet, a common theme in these methods is projecting the matrix into a different space and then transforming it back. I have seen this done with a matrix-log/matrix-exponential approach and also a less straightforward embedding map. 

One of the main applications of Lie regression is motion tracking. This generally fits with what we are trying to do and leads me to believe it is worth investigating further. It also uses a modeling framework that is familiar in statistics which makes it conceptually appealing for method extension.

My idea for injecting novelty into this method is adapting it for use with the correlated movement we see in teeth. At this stage, I cannot say for certain that no one has worked on this model with correlated responses, but it seems like an area that could be worth pursuing or pursuing in a way that has not yet been approached. 

As Grant has suggested in the past, starting with a simplified, simulated version of the data is a good starting point. In my head, I am imagining working this out in 2D space this triangles. The affine transformation matrix can also be decomposed into a rotation, scaling, and shear matrix: $T = RSH$. Parameters of each could be regressed separately. Additionally, an affine transformation can be minimally represented as Euler angles, an axis angle, a rotation vector, and a quaternion. Again, each piece could be handled separately.
